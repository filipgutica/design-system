Write a guide a reader can finish.

A guide answers one question for one reader. The page rail holds the site's pages; the list beside this column holds this page's sections, and it stays in view while you read.

Know the reader

Name who arrives at the page and what they already know. A guide for a new contributor starts further back than one for a maintainer.

Write down the one task they came to finish, and cut what does not serve it.

Lead with the first step

Put the first action near the top. A reader who can act at once keeps reading; one who meets background first often leaves.

Background that matters can follow the first step, in the section that needs it.

Show real examples

A worked example teaches faster than a rule. Use names and values a reader could meet, and keep each example small enough to read in one look.

When an example has a result, show the result too, so the reader can check their own.

Let the structure work

Headings name sections by what the reader does there. The contents list repeats them, so a heading that reads well alone also reads well in the list.

Keep sections in the order a reader meets the work, not the order the system was built.

Keep it short

Every sentence costs the reader time. Cut repeats, cut hedges, and move reference detail to the reference page, where the reader can look it up.

A short guide is easier to keep true, too.

Review it cold

Read the guide as the reader would, from a clean start. Follow each step exactly as written, and note where you had to guess.

Each guess is a gap: fill it in the guide, not in your head.

Ship and maintain

Publish the guide where readers look for it, and link it from the pages that lead there.

When the product changes, change the guide in the same change, so the two never drift apart.

Design language reference. Content and page names are illustrative.
